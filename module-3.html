Producing Data

Good inference begins with good data. Separate sampling, assignment, and bias.

1. Good inference needs good design

A poorly designed study can produce precise-looking but misleading results. Before calculating anything, ask who was studied, how subjects were selected, whether a treatment was imposed, and what population the results can reasonably describe.

  • Separate anecdotal evidence, observational studies, and experiments.
  • Recognize sampling designs and major sources of bias.
  • Identify factors, levels, treatments, and experimental units.
  • Choose a randomized design and assess control, blinding, and replication.

An observational study records explanatory and response variables without imposing treatments. It may be retrospective (look backward), cross-sectional (a present snapshot), or prospective (follow forward). An experiment imposes treatments and measures outcomes. A single friend’s experience is anecdotal evidence, not an adequate substitute for a well-designed study.

Worked example

Two cell-phone studies

  1. A questionnaire compares past phone use in 469 patients with brain cancer and 422 without: observational, because exposure was not assigned.
  2. A study exposes 100 mice to microwaves and leaves 100 unexposed: experimental, because exposure is imposed.
  3. Animal results may not generalize to humans. An ethically unacceptable human experiment does not become acceptable just because it would answer an important question.

2. How were subjects sampled?

A simple random sample (SRS) gives every possible sample of size n the same chance. Equal individual selection chances are necessary but alone do not guarantee an SRS. Random sampling supports generalization to the represented population.

DesignWhat you doKey distinction
SRSRandomly select from a frameAll samples equally likely
SystematicRandom start, every kth entryBeware periodic frame patterns
StratifiedSample within every stratumSome from every group
ClusterSample whole clustersAll from selected groups

Strata are groups whose members are similar with respect to a relevant characteristic, such as academic year. Clusters are often geographic or organizational units, ideally each reflecting population diversity. Stratification aims for representation; cluster sampling often saves travel and cost.

\text{systematic position }j=r+(j-1)k
Hand method · systematic sampling illustration

Every tenth name

  1. For interval k=10, randomly select a start from 1 through 10. Suppose r=4.
  2. Selected positions are 4, 14, 24, 34, …
  3. The sixth selected position is 4+(6−1)(10)=54.

3. Bias is not random variation

A sampling method is biased when it systematically favors some population members or responses. Undercoverage excludes people from the frame. Nonresponse bias occurs when selected people do not respond and differ in relevant ways from respondents. Response bias comes from inaccurate answers, social desirability, poor wording, or leading questions.

Convenience samples select easily available subjects. Voluntary response samples let subjects opt in. Neither reliably represents the target population. Increasing n reduces random sampling variability but does not remove systematic bias.

Worked example

A lot of replies can still mislead

Shere Hite’s survey was mailed to 100,000 women but received 4500 replies. Its size does not remove undercoverage or nonresponse concerns. A named paper survey can also elicit more socially desirable reports of study hours than an anonymous computer survey.

  1. Ask who had no chance to be sampled: undercoverage.
  2. Ask who was selected but did not reply: nonresponse.
  3. Ask whether recorded replies are accurate: response bias.
Red-pencil reminder. “Random sampling” and “random assignment” answer different questions. Sampling helps represent a population; assignment helps create comparable treatment groups.
More worked examples

Classic examples

  • Literary Digest, 1936: polled its own subscribers by phone and predicted Landon would beat Roosevelt. Roosevelt won in a landslide. Convenience sampling with severe undercoverage: in 1936, subscribers with phones were wealthier than voters overall.
  • "Would you have children again?" An advice column asked readers to write in; 70% of nearly 10,000 said no. Voluntary response: people with strong negative feelings were most motivated to reply.
  • Shere Hite's survey: mailed to 100,000 women, only 4,500 replied. Nonresponse bias: the 4.5% who answered may differ sharply from those who did not.
  • Paper versus laptop surveys: students randomly assigned to a signed paper survey reported more study hours than those answering anonymously on a laptop. Response bias: people give socially desirable answers when they can be identified.

4. Design an experiment

A factor is an imposed explanatory variable. Its values are levels. A treatment is a combination of levels. An experimental unit is the smallest unit receiving a treatment independently.

\text{number of treatments}=\prod \text{number of levels per factor}
Worked example

Soybeans, tillage, and pesticides

  1. Two tillage types and three pesticide schedules give 2×3=6 treatments: A1, A2, A3, B1, B2, B3.
  2. Ten fields each have six equal plots. Randomly assign the six treatments within each field.
  3. The experimental units are the 60 plots. Field location is a blocking variable, not a treatment: it already exists and is not imposed.
TI-842 × 3 gives six combinations. Random numbers can assign their order within each block.
Excel=2*3. For each field, attach RAND() values to the six treatments, sort, then freeze the assignment.

Completely randomized: assign units directly to treatment groups. Randomized block: form similar groups first, then randomize within each group. Matched pairs: match very similar subjects into pairs and randomize within pairs. Crossover: each subject receives both treatments in random order, with appropriate attention to carryover and washout.

In the running-shoe website example, putting comfort messages in the morning and discounts in the afternoon confounds treatment with time of day. Repeated use across ten days does not fix the lack of random assignment.

Watch for confounding. An online shoe store shows a comfort page in the mornings and a discount page in the afternoons, then compares sales. Time of day is confounded with the page shown, because the treatment was not randomized.

5. Four experimental essentials

  • Control: a comparison treatment, standard care, or placebo helps isolate the treatment effect. A placebo can address improvement from expectation alone.
  • Randomization: chance assignment balances known and unknown lurking variables in expectation. A single randomization does not guarantee perfectly identical groups.
  • Blinding: subjects do not know their assignment in a single-blind study; researchers in contact with subjects also do not know in a double-blind study.
  • Replication: apply each treatment to many experimental units to reduce random variation. This differs from reproducing an entire study.
Worked example

Zyban and smoking cessation

The 429 smokers are randomly assigned to Zyban or nothing. Randomization and replication are present, and the untreated group is a control. Blinding is limited because there is no placebo or alternative pill. Generalization is limited by the motivated, healthy volunteer group.

6. Scope and ethics

A well-conducted randomized comparative experiment supports causation in its setting. A representative random sample supports generalization. Neither result is automatic: nonresponse, noncompliance, measurement problems, and different populations can limit a study. Protect subjects through informed consent, confidentiality, and safeguards against harm. Evaluate what the study actually establishes before trusting a headline.
