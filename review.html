Module 1 review sheet

Data Distributions

Full notes · Practice · Quiz

From data to inference

Statistics is the art and science of learning from data.

Variables and displays

A variable is a characteristic recorded for each subject.

\hat p=\frac{X}{n},\qquad \text{percentage}=100\hat p\%
  • Watch out. Numbers are not always quantitative. A zip code or a jersey number is a label, so it is categorical. And a variable like "age group: 18–24, 25–34" is ordinal, even though it is built from numbers.

Center: mean and median

The mean uses every observation; the median is the midpoint of the ordered data; the mode is the most frequent value.

\bar x=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i}{n}
  • Common mistakes. Forgetting to sort before finding the median. With an even n, picking one of the two middle values instead of averaging them.

Spread: work out s by hand

The range is maximum minus minimum.

s^2=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1},\qquad s=\sqrt{s^2}
  • Red-pencil reminder. Divide by n−1 for a sample. Do not report variance when asked for SD. Neither the mean nor SD is resistant to outliers.
  • Watch out. The TI shows both Sx and σx. In this course you want Sx, which divides by n-1.

Quartiles, IQR, and outliers

Order the observations.

IQR=Q_3-Q_1,\quad \text{fences}=Q_1-1.5IQR,\;Q_3+1.5IQR
  • About outliers. Never delete an outlier just because it is extreme. Fix it if it is a typo; otherwise keep it in every calculation. If you report results without it, say so and say why.

Put the description together

Describe shape, center, spread, and outliers in context, including units.

Module 2 review sheet

Data Relationships

Full notes · Practice · Quiz

Ask how two variables relate

Bivariate analysis asks whether values of one variable occur more often with particular values of another.

Row proportions reveal association

Put the explanatory variable on rows.

\text{row proportion}=\frac{\text{cell count}}{\text{row total}}
  • Watch the denominator. Dividing 19485 by the grand total answers a joint question, not “among conventional foods.” Dividing by the pesticide column total reverses the condition.

Scatterplots and correlation

Describe a scatterplot’s direction, form, strength, and unusual observations before calculating.

R=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_i-\bar x}{s_x}\right)\left(\frac{y_i-\bar y}{s_y}\right)
  • Always look at the scatterplot. In the car data, adding one low outlier pulled R from -0.87 to -0.45, and a different outlier pushed it to -0.91. A curved pattern can also give an R near 0.

Least squares, by hand

A regression line predicts the response at a given explanatory value.

\hat y=b_0+b_1x,\quad b_1=R\frac{s_y}{s_x},\quad b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x,\quad e=y-\hat y

Fit, prediction, and influence

Interpolation predicts within the observed x range. Extrapolation predicts outside it and is risky: the observed relationship may not continue.

R^2=(R)^2=\text{proportion of variation in }y\text{ explained by regression on }x
  • Common mistakes. Computing \hat y - y instead of y - \hat y, which flips the sign of the residual. Swapping s_x and s_y in the slope formula.

Association and causation

Correlation does not imply causation.

Module 3 review sheet

Producing Data

Full notes · Practice · Quiz

Good inference needs good design

A poorly designed study can produce precise-looking but misleading results.

How were subjects sampled?

A simple random sample (SRS) gives every possible sample of size n the same chance.

\text{systematic position }j=r+(j-1)k

Bias is not random variation

A sampling method is biased when it systematically favors some population members or responses. Undercoverage excludes people from the frame. Nonresponse bias occurs when selected people do not respond and differ in relevant ways from respondents. Response bias comes from inaccurate answers, social desirability, poor wording, or leading questions.

  • Red-pencil reminder. “Random sampling” and “random assignment” answer different questions. Sampling helps represent a population; assignment helps create comparable treatment groups.

Design an experiment

A factor is an imposed explanatory variable.

\text{number of treatments}=\prod \text{number of levels per factor}
  • Watch for confounding. An online shoe store shows a comfort page in the mornings and a discount page in the afternoons, then compares sales. Time of day is confounded with the page shown, because the treatment was not randomized.

Four experimental essentials

The 429 smokers are randomly assigned to Zyban or nothing.

Scope and ethics

A well-conducted randomized comparative experiment supports causation in its setting.

Module 4 review sheet

Probability Basics

Full notes · Practice · Quiz

A language for chance

A random phenomenon has unpredictable individual outcomes but regular long-run behavior.

The probability rules

A union A∪B means A or B or both.

0\le P(A)\le1,\quad P(S)=1,\quad P(A^c)=1-P(A)
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)

“Given” changes the denominator

Conditioning restricts the sample space to an event already known to have occurred.

P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)},\quad P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)
  • Watch the denominator. A false positive is a positive result when the condition is absent. “Probability of a positive given no condition” differs from “probability of no condition given a positive.” Always name the condition after the vertical bar.
  • Common mistakes. Forgetting to subtract P(A\cap B) in the addition rule. Using the wrong denominator in a conditional probability: in P(B\mid A), the denominator is the "given" group A.

Independence and repeated trials

Independent events do not change one another’s probabilities.

A\perp B\iff P(B\mid A)=P(B)\iff P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)
P(\text{at least one success})=1-(1-p)^n

Multiply branches, then add paths

On a probability tree, probabilities leaving each node sum to 1.

Discrete and continuous models

A discrete random variable X has countable outcomes; add their probabilities for an event.

\mu_X=\sum xp(x),\quad\sigma_X^2=\sum(x-\mu_X)^2p(x),\quad\sigma_X=\sqrt{\sigma_X^2}
P(c<X<d)=\frac{d-c}{b-a}\quad(a\le c<d\le b)
  • Common mistakes. Forgetting to multiply by p_i when finding the mean or variance. Reporting the variance when the standard deviation was asked. Treating X > 2 as if it included 2.
Module 5 review sheet

Normal & Binomial Distributions

Full notes · Practice · Quiz

Recognize a normal model

A normal distribution is symmetric, unimodal, and bell-shaped.

68\%:\mu\pm\sigma,\qquad95\%:\mu\pm2\sigma,\qquad99.7\%:\mu\pm3\sigma

Standardize before the table

A z-score tells how many SDs an observation lies above (positive) or below (negative) its mean.

z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma},\qquad Z\sim N(0,1)

Four normal-area patterns

Let Φ(z) denote the left area from the normal table.

P(Z>a)=1-\Phi(a),\quad P(a<Z<b)=\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)
P(Z<a\text{ or }Z>b)=\Phi(a)+1-\Phi(b)
  • Draw first. A “greater than” question usually needs a complement. A “between” question needs subtraction of two left areas. Do not read one table entry and stop.
  • Common mistakes. Reporting the table value when the question asks for "greater than." Subtracting in the wrong order for "between" (the answer can't be negative). Forgetting to standardize and looking up x itself in the table.

Work backward: inverse normal

When an area is given, first express it as an area to the left.

x=\mu+z\sigma

Is it binomial? Check BINS

B: binary outcome on each trial. I: independent trials. N: a fixed number n of trials. S: the same success probability p on every trial.

P(X=k)=\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
  • Small populations break independence. Choosing 6 of your 20 friends, 4 of them left-handed: the chance of a lefty is 4/20 on the first pick but 3/19 or 4/19 on the next. Rule of thumb: the sample should be less than 5% of the population (population at least 20 times the sample).
  • Keep four decimals (or more) in each term and round only at the end. Your calculator's MATH PRB 3:nCr computes \binom{n}{k}: type 30 nCr 2.

Binomial center, spread, approximation

Use the normal approximation only when np≥10 and n(1−p)≥10.

\mu=np,\quad\sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)},\quad X\approx N\!\left(np,\sqrt{np(1-p)}\right)
Module 6 review sheet

Sampling Distributions

Full notes · Practice · Quiz

Three distinct distributions

This module connects probability to inference.

\hat p=\frac{X}{n},\qquad \bar x=\frac{\sum x_i}{n}

Sampling distribution of p̂

A sample count X and a sample proportion p̂ describe the same successes on different scales.

\mu_{\hat p}=p,\quad\sigma_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}n}

Probabilities and middle ranges for p̂

p=.40 and n=96.

z=\frac{\hat p-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}
  • Red-pencil reminder. The denominator is the SD of p̂, √[p(1−p)/n]. Do not divide by p, 1−p, or the SD of the success count.
  • Key step. Standardize with \sigma_{\hat p}, the standard deviation of the sampling distribution. Dividing by \sqrt{p(1-p)} without the n, or by p, gives the wrong answer.

Sample means and the CLT

For random, independent observations, these center and spread formulas hold regardless of population shape when the population variance is finite.

\mu_{\bar x}=\mu,\qquad\sigma_{\bar x}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}

Use the spread of the mean

Individual wingspans follow N(4,.25).

z=\frac{\bar x-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}
  • Do not reuse σ. Using .25 instead of .125 would give z=.80 and the incorrect .2119. A mean of four has less variability than one observation.

Center stays; spread shrinks

p̂ and x̄ are unbiased: their sampling distributions are centered at p and μ.

  • Quadruple the sample, halve the spread. Because \sigma_{\bar x} = \sigma/\sqrt n, multiplying n by 4 divides the standard deviation by \sqrt4 = 2. The same is true for \hat p.
Module 7 review sheet

Confidence Intervals

Full notes · Practice · Quiz

Estimate a fixed population parameter

A point estimate is a single best guess; a confidence interval adds a margin to describe precision.

\text{confidence interval}=\text{point estimate}\pm\text{margin of error}

Confidence interval for p

Conditions: random data, approximate independence, at least 10 observed successes and 10 observed failures.

SE_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n},\quad\hat p\pm z^*SE_{\hat p}
  • Use the right counts. For a proportion CI, check successes and failures in the observed sample. For a proportion test in Module 8, use expected counts under p₀ instead.
  • Common mistakes. Using z^* for the wrong confidence level. Leaving n out of the square root. Rounding \hat p or the SE too early; store them instead.

Why t replaces z for a mean

Population σ is usually unknown, so estimate it with sample s.

SE_{\bar x}=\frac{s}{\sqrt n},\quad T=\frac{\bar x-\mu}{s/\sqrt n},\quad df=n-1

Confidence interval for μ

Conditions: randomly gathered, approximately independent data, and no extreme skew or extreme outliers.

\bar x\pm t^*\frac{s}{\sqrt n}

Margin, width, and interpretation

For (.40,.56), the center is .48, width .16, and margin .08.

\text{estimate}=\frac{L+U}{2},\quad m=\frac{U-L}{2},\quad\text{width}=2m
  • Wrong interpretations to avoid. "There is a 95% probability that p is in (0.40, 0.56)." "95% of the data fall in the interval." "95% of sample proportions fall in this interval." The interval is about the parameter, and the 95% is about the method.

Plan precision before collecting data

For p, use a pilot p̂ or the conservative .5 if no estimate is available.

n_p=\left\lceil\frac{(z^*)^2\hat p(1-\hat p)}{m^2}\right\rceil,\qquad n_\mu\approx\left\lceil\frac{4s^2}{m^2}\right\rceil\;\text{at 95\%}
Module 8 review sheet

Hypothesis Testing + Uses & Abuses

Full notes · Practice · Quiz

A claim, then evidence

Earlier modules estimated a parameter with a point estimate and a confidence interval.

H_0:p=p_0\ \text{ or }\ H_0:\mu=\mu_0;\qquad H_a:\ >,\ <,\ \text{or}\ \ne
\text{TS}=\frac{\text{estimate}-\text{hypothesized value of parameter}}{\text{standard error of estimate}}
  • Claims with “at least” or “at most”. Management claims mean income is at least $500. That is the status quo, so it goes in H_0: \mu = 500, and the suspicion goes in H_a: \mu < 500. In this course H_0 is always written with “=”. Use the parameters p and μ, never the statistics p̂ and x̄.

Critical values and the rejection region

We need an objective rule for deciding when a test statistic is extreme enough to reject H₀.

  • Two ways to decide. The rejection region is one way to make the decision. The next section shows the other, the p-value approach. Both always give the same decision, so on a quiz or exam you may use either one.

The p-value approach

Instead of asking whether the test statistic lands in the rejection region, ask: if H₀ were true, how surprising is a test statistic this extreme?

\text{Reject } H_0 \text{ if p-value} \le \alpha;\quad \text{otherwise fail to reject } H_0.
  • Common mistakes. Forgetting to double the tail area for a two-sided test. Reading the left area when Hₐ has > (subtract from 1). Comparing the p-value with α/2 instead of α (the doubling already accounts for both tails). Saying the p-value is the probability that H₀ is true: it is the probability of data this extreme if H₀ is true.

Test a single proportion

Hypotheses. H_0:p=p_0 versus one of H_a:p>p_0, H_a:p<p_0 (one-sided) or H_a:p\ne p_0 (two-sided), where p is the unknown population proportion and p₀ its hypothesized value.

\text{TS}=z=\frac{\hat p-p_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}}\qquad\text{TS}\sim N(0,1)
  • Common mistakes. Using p̂ in the standard error (a test assumes H₀, so it uses p₀; only a confidence interval uses p̂). Using column α instead of α/2 for a two-sided test. Forgetting the minus sign for a lower-tailed test: reject when TS ≤ −z*. Saying “accept H₀”: we only fail to reject it.

Test a single mean with t

So far every example used categorical data, a sample proportion p̂ and the Z distribution.

\text{TS}=t=\frac{\bar x-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n}\qquad \text{TS}\sim t_{n-1}
  • Common mistakes. Using df = n instead of n − 1. Rounding df up to the next listed row (always use the next smaller df). Comparing a negative TS with +t* in a lower-tailed test. Using z* for a mean when σ is unknown. Forgetting to double a two-sided p-value.

Errors, α, β, and power

When choosing a sample size, consider the significance level (how much protection against a Type I error), the effect size (how large a difference matters in practice: |p_a-p_0| for a proportion, |\mu_a-\mu_0| for a mean), and the power (how sure we want to be of detecting that effect).

\text{Power}=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ is false})=1-\beta

Significance, usefulness, and CIs

A test has statistical significance when it leads us to reject H₀ in favor of Hₐ, that is, when the test statistic falls in the rejection region.

  • Read the strength of the claim. Failing to reject does not show H₀ is true; the test may simply lack power. Do not choose hypotheses or α after seeing the data, keep testing until something lands in the rejection region, or report only favorable results.
