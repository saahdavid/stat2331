1. Three distinct distributions

This module connects probability to inference. A statistic changes from sample to sample even when the population and sample size stay the same. A sampling distribution describes that repeated-sampling variation.

  • Population distribution: individual values in the population, summarized by parameters.
  • Data distribution: individual values in one observed sample, summarized by statistics.
  • Sampling distribution: the values of a statistic across all possible repeated samples of a fixed size.

Our goals are to find the center, spread, and shape of sampling distributions; check normal-approximation conditions; and calculate probabilities and middle ranges for p̂ and x̄. Increasing n changes the sampling distribution, not the original population’s shape.

\hat p=\frac{X}{n},\qquad \bar x=\frac{\sum x_i}{n}

2. Sampling distribution of p̂

A sample count X and a sample proportion p̂ describe the same successes on different scales. If n=20, possible proportions are 0, .05, .10, …, 1. The mean of repeated sample proportions is p. Their SD gets smaller as n increases.

\mu_{\hat p}=p,\quad\sigma_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}n}

For random, approximately independent observations, p̂ is approximately normal when np≥10 and n(1−p)≥10. If those conditions fail, calculate probabilities through the binomial count X instead. For sampling without replacement, our course uses the population-at-least-20n rule of thumb to justify approximate independence.

Worked example

Why n=20 is not quite enough

The claimed preference proportion is p=.57.

  1. For n=20, μp̂=.57 and σp̂=√[.57(.43)/20]=.1107.
  2. np=11.4, but n(1−p)=8.6. One condition fails.
  3. Solve both: n≥10/.57=17.54… and n≥10/.43=23.26…
  4. Use the larger bound, then round up: n≥24.
  5. At n=30, σp̂=.0904… and the normal approximation is appropriate.

Excel=SQRT(0.57*0.43/30); =ROUNDUP(MAX(10/0.57,10/0.43),0).
Worked example

A pizzeria's claim

A Boston pizzeria claims 57% of Bostonians prefer its pizza. In one random sample of 20, 13 (65%) do. With n = 20, \hat p can be 0, 0.05, 0.10, …, 1. A thousand different samples of 20 would give many different \hat p values. Simulating that, the 1000 values of \hat p averaged 0.57 with a standard deviation of 0.11. With samples of 50, they still averaged 0.57, but the standard deviation shrank to 0.07.

3. Probabilities and middle ranges for p̂

z=\frac{\hat p-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}
Worked example

Owls in saguaro cacti

p=.40 and n=96. Find P(p̂<.27).

  1. Check np=38.4 and n(1−p)=57.6: both pass.
  2. Center=.40; SD=√[.40(.60)/96]=.05.
  3. z=(.27−.40)/.05=−2.60.
  4. Left-area table: P(p̂<.27)=Φ(−2.60)=.0047.
  5. The middle 80% uses z*=1.282: .40±1.282(.05)=(.3359,.4641). Rounding z* to 1.28 gives (.336,.464).
TI-84normalcdf(−1E99,.27,.4,.05). Middle range: invNorm(.1,.4,.05) and invNorm(.9,.4,.05).
Excel=NORM.DIST(0.27,0.4,0.05,TRUE). Endpoints: =NORM.INV(0.1,0.4,0.05) and the .9 quantile.

For the pizzeria at n=30, standardize .50 and .60 with the unrounded SD. Rounded z values are −.77 and .33. The table probability is .6293−.2206=.4087, while software gives .4107. That difference is due to z rounding.

Red-pencil reminder. The denominator is the SD of p̂, √[p(1−p)/n]. Do not divide by p, 1−p, or the SD of the success count.
Worked example

Probability for \hat p

With p = 0.57 and n = 30, what is P(0.50 < \hat p < 0.60)?

  1. \mu_{\hat p} = 0.57 and \sigma_{\hat p} = \sqrt{0.57(0.43)/30} = 0.0904. (Conditions: 30(0.57)=17.1 and 30(0.43) = 12.9, both at least 10.)
  2. z_1 = (0.50-0.57)/0.0904 = -0.77 and z_2 = (0.60 - 0.57)/0.0904 = 0.33.
  3. P = 0.6293 - 0.2206 = 0.4087. Software gives 0.4107; the difference is rounding z.
Key step. Standardize with \sigma_{\hat p}, the standard deviation of the sampling distribution. Dividing by \sqrt{p(1-p)} without the n, or by p, gives the wrong answer.

4. Sample means and the CLT

\mu_{\bar x}=\mu,\qquad\sigma_{\bar x}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}

For random, independent observations, these center and spread formulas hold regardless of population shape when the population variance is finite. Shape requires an additional argument:

  • If the population is normal, x̄ is exactly normal for any n.
  • If the population is not normal, the CLT gives an approximately normal x̄ for sufficiently large n. Our course guideline is n≥30; strong skew and extreme outliers can require more.
  • If the population is not normal and n<30, normality of x̄ is not established.

For example, weekly beer counts can be very right skewed. Averaging many observations makes the distribution of means less skewed and narrower. It does not transform individual beer counts into normal data.

Worked example

When we can't use the normal model

The number of people students dated in the past month has \mu = 0.854, \sigma = 1.024, and is extremely right-skewed. With n = 10, the population isn't normal and n < 30, so the sampling distribution of \bar x is unknown. We cannot find P(\bar x > 2) with the normal table.

5. Use the spread of the mean

z=\frac{\bar x-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}
Worked example

Mean wingspan of four dragonflies

Individual wingspans follow N(4,.25). Take an SRS of four.

  1. Since the population is normal, x̄ is exactly normal.
  2. μx̄=4; σx̄=.25/√4=.125.
  3. For x̄>4.2, z=(4.2−4)/.125=1.60.
  4. P(x̄>4.2)=1−.9452=.0548.
TI-84normalcdf(4.2,1E99,4,.125).
Excel=1-NORM.DIST(4.2,4,0.25/SQRT(4),TRUE).
Do not reuse σ. Using .25 instead of .125 would give z=.80 and the incorrect .2119. A mean of four has less variability than one observation.
Worked example

IQ for children with FAS

The population has μ=70, σ=12. Take n=36.

  1. The course CLT guideline applies, since n≥30.
  2. σx̄=12/√36=2.
  3. For 64<x̄<71, z₁=−3.00 and z₂=.50.
  4. Table area=.6915−.0013=.6902; software=.6901.
  5. The middle 95% of means is 70±1.96(2)=(66.08,73.92).

6. Center stays; spread shrinks

p̂ and x̄ are unbiased: their sampling distributions are centered at p and μ. Unbiased does not mean every sample estimate equals the parameter. Increasing n reduces spread. Multiplying n by four halves the SD; multiplying n by nine divides it by three. Do not confuse the size of each sample with the number of simulated samples: the former changes the theoretical SD, while the latter improves how clearly a simulation reveals it.

Based on Module 6 lecture notes. Conditions and scale distinctions are made explicit for hand calculations.

Quadruple the sample, halve the spread. Because \sigma_{\bar x} = \sigma/\sqrt n, multiplying n by 4 divides the standard deviation by \sqrt4 = 2. The same is true for \hat p.
