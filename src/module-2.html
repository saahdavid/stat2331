1. Ask how two variables relate

Bivariate analysis asks whether values of one variable occur more often with particular values of another. An explanatory variable x predicts or defines comparison groups; a response y is the outcome. The word “explanatory” alone does not establish a causal relationship.

  • Choose displays and summaries appropriate to both variable types.
  • Interpret correlation, a regression line, residuals, and R².
  • Calculate a regression line from summary statistics.
  • Recognize extrapolation, influential points, and lurking variables.

For the pet-and-stress example (lecture p. 6), the pet group’s mean heart rate is 73.48, versus 91.33 with a friend and 82.52 alone. The pet group has the lowest mean and median. Compare groups on a shared scale so graphical differences remain meaningful.

More worked examples
  • Smoking (yes/no) and survival after 20 years (yes/no): smoking is explanatory, survival is the response; both categorical.
  • Fruit type (A, B, C) and sucrose level in grams: fruit type is explanatory and categorical; sucrose is the response and quantitative.
  • Gasoline used daily and air pollution in ppm: gasoline is explanatory; pollution is the response; both quantitative.

2. Row proportions reveal association

Put the explanatory variable on rows. Within each row, divide each cell by the row total. Differences in these conditional proportions indicate association. Raw counts can mislead when groups have very different sizes.

\text{row proportion}=\frac{\text{cell count}}{\text{row total}}

3. Scatterplots and correlation

Describe a scatterplot’s direction, form, strength, and unusual observations before calculating. R summarizes direction and strength of a linear relationship. Its sign is the direction; its magnitude shows how tightly points follow a line. R is unitless, between −1 and 1, and unchanged by switching x and y or by changing measurement units through positive linear rescaling.

R=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_i-\bar x}{s_x}\right)\left(\frac{y_i-\bar y}{s_y}\right)

R = 0 means no linear association, but a strong curved relationship may still exist. R = ±1 means all points lie on a line. Outliers can greatly change R. The vehicle example has R ≈ −0.866: a strong negative linear association between weight and MPG.

TI-84Enter x in L1 and y in L2. Turn DiagnosticOn on in 2nd 0 (CATALOG), then run LinReg(a+bx).
Excel=CORREL(A2:A26,B2:B26). Plot the paired values first.
Always look at the scatterplot. In the car data, adding one low outlier pulled R from -0.87 to -0.45, and a different outlier pushed it to -0.91. A curved pattern can also give an R near 0.

4. Least squares, by hand

A regression line predicts the response at a given explanatory value. The least-squares line minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals. A residual is observed minus predicted. Positive residuals lie above the line; negative ones lie below. With an intercept, the least-squares line passes through (x̄, ȳ) and its residuals sum to zero.

\hat y=b_0+b_1x,\quad b_1=R\frac{s_y}{s_x},\quad b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x,\quad e=y-\hat y
Hand method · summary-statistic illustration

From summaries to an equation

Suppose x̄=10, ȳ=30, sx=2, sy=6, R=0.8.

  1. Slope: b₁=0.8(6/2)=2.4 response units per x unit.
  2. Intercept: b₀=30−2.4(10)=6.
  3. Line: ŷ=6+2.4x. At x=12, prediction is 34.8.
  4. If y=32, the residual is 32−34.8=−2.8, below the line.

5. Fit, prediction, and influence

Interpolation predicts within the observed x range. Extrapolation predicts outside it and is risky: the observed relationship may not continue. The infant example’s line, ŷ=−932.404+70.310x, is used for gestational ages 23–35 weeks. At 30 weeks, predicted birth weight is 1176.896 grams.

Inspect the residual plot. A satisfactory straight-line fit gives an unstructured horizontal band about zero. A curve suggests a missing nonlinear pattern; fanning suggests changing variability. A large R² does not repair these problems.

R^2=(R)^2=\text{proportion of variation in }y\text{ explained by regression on }x

For the vehicle example, R²≈0.751: about 75.1% of variation in MPG is explained by the linear regression on weight. It is not the percentage of points exactly on the line or a probability that the model is true.

An influential point substantially changes the fitted line. An extreme x value can have high influence even if its residual is not initially large. Compare a fit with and without the point; investigate whether it is a recording error, but do not automatically delete it.

Excel=RSQ(y_range,x_range). Calculate residuals as observed y minus predicted y, then scatterplot them against x.
Common mistakes. Computing \hat y - y instead of y - \hat y, which flips the sign of the residual. Swapping s_x and s_y in the slope formula.
Worked example

Two models for a depression index (DPI)

Work satisfaction gives R^2 = 0.709; social interaction gives R^2 = 0.844.

  • Interpretation: 70.9% of the variability in DPI is explained by the linear regression on work satisfaction.
  • The social index model is better because its R^2 is higher. A model using both variables might do better still.

6. Association and causation

Correlation does not imply causation. A lurking variable is not included in the analysis but affects its interpretation. Town population can increase both the number of churches and the number of bars (common response). Temperature can increase both ice cream sales and swimming, creating a relationship with drowning deaths. Confounding makes the separate effects difficult to distinguish.

Choose a model using subject knowledge, the scatterplot, residual behavior, and explanatory power together. Write predictions and residuals with units, interpret slopes in context, and treat extrapolation cautiously.
