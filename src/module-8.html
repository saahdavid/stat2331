1. A claim, then evidence

Earlier modules estimated a parameter with a point estimate and a confidence interval. Now we use probability to test a claim about a parameter such as a proportion p or a mean μ. A hypothesis is a claim that a population parameter takes a particular value or falls in a range of values.

  • Null hypothesis H₀: the statement of no effect, assumed true until shown to be false. We look for evidence against it.
  • Alternative hypothesis Hₐ: the statement we hope to demonstrate. We look for evidence for it.
  • State both hypotheses (and α) before gathering or analyzing data. Choosing them after seeing the data is unethical.
H_0:p=p_0\ \text{ or }\ H_0:\mu=\mu_0;\qquad H_a:\ >,\ <,\ \text{or}\ \ne

We cannot prove either hypothesis without a census; we can only weigh the evidence. The test is a proof by contradiction: assume H₀ is true, collect data, and ask whether the data contradict that assumption. It works like a court case: the defendant is presumed innocent (H₀) unless there is evidence of guilt (Hₐ) beyond a reasonable doubt. If the data are consistent with H₀, or give little support to Hₐ, we fail to reject H₀. If the data strongly support Hₐ, we reject H₀.

Claims with “at least” or “at most”. Management claims mean income is at least $500. That is the status quo, so it goes in H_0: \mu = 500, and the suspicion goes in H_a: \mu < 500. In this course H_0 is always written with “=”. Use the parameters p and μ, never the statistics p̂ and x̄.
Worked example

Therapeutic touch: setting up the test

Ten blindfolded therapeutic-touch (TT) practitioners each had a researcher hold her hand over one of their hands, chosen at random, 10 times. They identified the correct hand 52 times out of 100.

  1. Parameter: p = the true proportion of times a TT practitioner can perceive the energy field (picks the correct hand).
  2. If they are just guessing, they will be right about 50% of the time.
  3. H_0: p = 0.5 (at most half the time, just guessing); H_a: p > 0.5 (more than half the time).
  4. The data lean toward Hₐ because \hat p = 52/100 = 0.52 > 0.5. But is 0.52 far enough above 0.5? We need to measure the strength of the evidence: if H₀ were true, how unusual would a sample like this be?

Test statistics

A test statistic (TS) counts how many standard errors the estimate is from the hypothesized value of the parameter.

\text{TS}=\frac{\text{estimate}-\text{hypothesized value of parameter}}{\text{standard error of estimate}}

A TS close to 0 means the data are compatible with H₀. An extreme TS (far from 0) means the data are not compatible with H₀. If the TS is extreme, either H₀ is true and we drew a very unusual sample (possible but unlikely), or H₀ is false (more plausible, and what statisticians choose to believe).

2. Critical values and the rejection region

We need an objective rule for deciding when a test statistic is extreme enough to reject H₀.

  • The rejection region is the set of test-statistic values so extreme they would be unlikely if H₀ were true.
  • Its boundary is the critical value, z^* or t^*, read from the t-table.
  • The area of the rejection region equals the significance level α.

How to find it. First assume H₀ is true: this gives the distribution of the test statistic (Z for a proportion, t_{n-1} for a mean). Then find the critical value that cuts off area α in the direction of Hₐ.

  • TS in the rejection region: the data are too unusual to believe H₀, so reject H₀. We have not proven anything, but there is statistically significant evidence at the α level for Hₐ.
  • TS not in the rejection region: the data are consistent with H₀, so fail to reject H₀. There is not enough evidence at the α level for Hₐ.

The significance level α

Decide in advance how much evidence against H₀ you require. The common levels are 0.10, 0.05 and 0.01, with α = 0.05 the default. Setting α = 0.05 means we require evidence so strong it would happen less than 5% of the time if H₀ were true.

Finding critical values with the t-table

  • Columns give the upper-tail probability. One-sided test: use column α. Two-sided test: use column α/2.
  • Rows give the degrees of freedom. Mean: row df=n-1, critical value t^*. Proportion: the bottom z^* row. If your df is not listed, use the next smaller df in the table.
  • Sign: Hₐ with >: reject if \text{TS} \ge \text{critical value}. Hₐ with <: reject if \text{TS} \le -\text{critical value}. Hₐ with \ne: reject if |\text{TS}| \ge \text{critical value}.

Worked example

Reading critical values

  1. Proportion, Hₐ: p > p₀, α = 0.01: z* row, column 0.01, so z^*=2.326. Reject if TS ≥ 2.326.
  2. Proportion, Hₐ: p ≠ p₀, α = 0.05: z* row, column α/2 = 0.025, so z^*=1.960. Reject if |TS| ≥ 1.960.
  3. Mean, n = 8, Hₐ: μ < μ₀, α = 0.05: row df = 7, column 0.05, so t^*=1.895. Reject if TS ≤ −1.895.
  4. Mean, n = 165, Hₐ: μ ≠ μ₀, α = 0.01: df = 164 is not in the table, so use the next smaller row, df = 100; column 0.005 gives t^*=2.626. Reject if |TS| ≥ 2.626.

Two ways to decide. The rejection region is one way to make the decision. The next section shows the other, the p-value approach. Both always give the same decision, so on a quiz or exam you may use either one.

3. The p-value approach

Instead of asking whether the test statistic lands in the rejection region, ask: if H₀ were true, how surprising is a test statistic this extreme?

  • The p-value is the probability, assuming H₀ is true, of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed, in the direction of Hₐ.
  • A small p-value means data like ours would be rare if H₀ were true: strong evidence against H₀.
  • A large p-value means data like ours are common under H₀: little evidence against it.
The p-value follows the direction of Hₐ (use Z for a proportion, T with df = n − 1 for a mean)
Alternativep-valueArea
H_a: p>p_0 or \mu>\mu_0P(Z \ge \text{TS})to the right of TS
H_a: p<p_0 or \mu<\mu_0P(Z \le \text{TS})to the left of TS
H_a: p\ne p_0 or \mu\ne\mu_02P(Z \ge |\text{TS}|)both tails beyond \pm|\text{TS}|
\text{Reject } H_0 \text{ if p-value} \le \alpha;\quad \text{otherwise fail to reject } H_0.

The conclusion is written exactly as before: there [is / is not] sufficient evidence at the α level that the parameter is [greater than / less than / different from] the null value.

Finding the p-value for a proportion (normal table)

Round z to two decimals and read the left area from the standard normal table.

  • Hₐ with >: p-value = 1 − (left area at z).
  • Hₐ with <: p-value = left area at z.
  • Hₐ with ≠: p-value = 2 × (1 − left area at |z|). Do not forget to double.
  • Beyond ±3.49 the table runs out: the tail area is essentially 0, so the p-value is less than 0.0001.

Finding the p-value for a mean (t-table gives an interval)

The t-table lists only a few tail areas, so by hand you can only say which two areas the p-value falls between.

  1. Go to row df=n-1 (the next smaller df if yours is not listed).
  2. Find the two table values that |\text{TS}| falls between. The column headings above them are the two tail areas.
  3. One-sided test: the p-value is between those two areas. Two-sided test: double both.
  4. If |\text{TS}| is beyond the last column, the p-value is less than 0.0005 (less than 0.001 for a two-sided test). If it is below the first column, the p-value is more than 0.25 (more than 0.5 two-sided).

That interval is enough to decide whenever α is not inside it. The exact p-value comes from software: on the TI, \texttt{tcdf}; in Excel, T.DIST.

Worked example

Reading p-values from the tables

  1. Proportion, Hₐ: p > 0.5, z = 0.40. The left area at 0.40 is 0.6554, so p-value = 1 − 0.6554 = 0.3446.
  2. Proportion, Hₐ: p ≠ 0.3, z = −2.10. The left area at −2.10 is 0.0179, so p-value = 2(0.0179) = 0.0358.
  3. Mean, Hₐ: μ < 5, t = −3.204, n = 8. Row df = 7: 3.204 is between 2.998 (column 0.01) and 3.499 (column 0.005), so 0.005 < p-value < 0.01. Exactly, p-value = 0.0075.
  4. Mean, Hₐ: μ ≠ 30, t = 2.20, n = 20. Row df = 19: 2.20 is between 2.093 (column 0.025) and 2.205 (column 0.02). Two-sided, so double: 0.04 < p-value < 0.05. At α = 0.05, the p-value is below α, so reject H₀.
TI-84 check2ND → DISTR. normalcdf(0.4,1E99) = .3446; tcdf(-1E99,-3.204,7) = .0075. For a two-sided test, double the one-tail area.
Excel check=1-NORM.S.DIST(0.4,TRUE) gives 0.3446; =T.DIST(-3.204,7,TRUE) gives 0.0075; =T.DIST.2T(2.2,19) gives 0.0404.

Why the two methods always agree

The rejection region is the tail with area α. The test statistic lands in it exactly when the area beyond the test statistic, the p-value, is at most α. In every worked example below, use the Decide with switch on the graph to watch both methods reach the same decision.

Common mistakes. Forgetting to double the tail area for a two-sided test. Reading the left area when Hₐ has > (subtract from 1). Comparing the p-value with α/2 instead of α (the doubling already accounts for both tails). Saying the p-value is the probability that H₀ is true: it is the probability of data this extreme if H₀ is true.
Not sure which method a problem needs? Use the method guide: three questions point to the right interval, test, or sample-size formula, and the drill gives you practice recognizing each one.

4. Test a single proportion

Hypotheses. H_0:p=p_0 versus one of H_a:p>p_0, H_a:p<p_0 (one-sided) or H_a:p\ne p_0 (two-sided), where p is the unknown population proportion and p₀ its hypothesized value.

Assumptions. Normality: both np_0\ge 10 and n(1-p_0)\ge 10 (the expected numbers of successes and failures if H₀ is true). Independence: randomly gathered data.

\text{TS}=z=\frac{\hat p-p_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}}\qquad\text{TS}\sim N(0,1)

Rejection region. If Hₐ: p > p₀, reject H₀ if TS ≥ z*. If Hₐ: p < p₀, reject if TS ≤ −z*. If Hₐ: p ≠ p₀, reject if |TS| ≥ z*. The critical value z* comes from the bottom row of the t-table, column α (one-sided) or α/2 (two-sided).

Decision and conclusion. If the TS falls in the rejection region, reject H₀; otherwise fail to reject (FTR) H₀. Then write: “There is / is not sufficient evidence at the α level that the proportion of [variable of interest] is [greater than / less than / different from] p₀.” Always state the conclusion in terms of Hₐ.

Worked example

Can TT practitioners perceive an energy field? \alpha=0.01

  1. Hypotheses: H_0:p=0.5, H_a:p>0.5.
  2. Assumptions: np_0=n(1-p_0)=100(0.5)=50\ge 10; the hand was chosen at random each time.
  3. Test statistic: z=\dfrac{0.52-0.5}{\sqrt{0.5(0.5)/100}}=\dfrac{0.02}{0.05}=0.4.
  4. Critical value: upper-tailed, α = 0.01, z* row, column 0.01, so z^*=2.326. Reject H₀ if TS ≥ 2.326.
  5. Decision: TS = 0.4 < 2.326 is not in the rejection region, so we fail to reject H₀.
  6. Or with the p-value: P(Z\ge 0.40)=1-0.6554=0.3446, and 0.3446 > α = 0.01, so we fail to reject H₀. Same decision.
  7. Conclusion: there is insufficient evidence at the α = 0.01 level that the true proportion of times a TT practitioner can perceive a human energy field is greater than 0.5.
TI-84 checkSTAT → TESTS → 5:1-PropZTest: p₀ = .5, x = 52, n = 100, prop > p₀. It reports z = .4 (and a p-value of .3446 > .01, the same decision).
Excel check=(52/100-0.5)/SQRT(0.5*0.5/100) gives 0.4; =NORM.S.INV(0.99) gives 2.326.
Worked example

Can humans really randomize? \alpha=0.05

A random sample of 189 people chose a “random” number from 1 to 10. If choices were random, each number would be picked 10% of the time, but 69 chose 7. Is the probability of choosing 7 different from 10%?

  1. Hypotheses: H_0:p=0.1, H_a:p\ne 0.1.
  2. Assumptions: np_0=189(0.1)=18.9\ge 10 and n(1-p_0)=189(0.9)=170.1\ge 10; a random sample.
  3. Test statistic: z=\dfrac{69/189-0.1}{\sqrt{0.1(0.9)/189}}=12.15.
  4. Critical value: two-sided, α = 0.05, z* row, column α/2 = 0.025, so z^*=1.960. Reject H₀ if |TS| ≥ 1.960.
  5. Decision: TS = 12.15 ≥ 1.960 is in the rejection region, so we reject H₀.
  6. Or with the p-value: 2P(Z\ge 12.15) is far beyond the table, so the p-value is less than 0.0001, well below α = 0.05: reject H₀. Same decision.
  7. Conclusion: there is sufficient evidence at the 0.05 level that the probability a person chooses 7 from the numbers 1 through 10 is not 0.1.
Worked example

Are pro-choice adults in the minority? \alpha=0.05

A Gallup poll of 1,014 randomly selected adults found 48% consider themselves pro-choice. One group used this as evidence that pro-choice adults are a minority.

  1. Hypotheses: H_0:p=0.50, H_a:p<0.50.
  2. Assumptions: np_0=n(1-p_0)=1014(0.5)=507\ge 10; a random sample.
  3. Test statistic: z=\dfrac{0.48-0.50}{\sqrt{0.5(0.5)/1014}}=-1.2737.
  4. Critical value: lower-tailed, α = 0.05, z* row, column 0.05, so z^*=1.645. Reject H₀ if TS ≤ −1.645.
  5. Decision: TS = −1.2737 > −1.645 is not in the rejection region, so we fail to reject H₀.
  6. Or with the p-value: round to z = −1.27; P(Z\le -1.27)=0.1020, and 0.1020 > α = 0.05, so we fail to reject H₀. Same decision.
  7. Conclusion: there is insufficient evidence at the 0.05 level that the proportion of adults who are pro-choice is less than 0.5. A sample proportion below 0.5 is not, by itself, enough.
Common mistakes. Using p̂ in the standard error (a test assumes H₀, so it uses p₀; only a confidence interval uses p̂). Using column α instead of α/2 for a two-sided test. Forgetting the minus sign for a lower-tailed test: reject when TS ≤ −z*. Saying “accept H₀”: we only fail to reject it.

5. Test a single mean with t

So far every example used categorical data, a sample proportion p̂ and the Z distribution. For quantitative data we use the sample mean x̄ and the t_{n-1} distribution to make inference about the population mean μ.

Hypotheses. H_0:\mu=\mu_0 versus H_a:\mu>\mu_0, H_a:\mu<\mu_0 or H_a:\mu\ne\mu_0.

Assumptions. Normality: no extreme skew or extreme outliers (check a histogram, boxplot or normal quantile plot; t procedures depend on x̄ and s, which are strongly affected by them). Independence: randomly gathered data.

\text{TS}=t=\frac{\bar x-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n}\qquad \text{TS}\sim t_{n-1}

Rejection region. If Hₐ: μ > μ₀, reject H₀ if TS ≥ t*. If Hₐ: μ < μ₀, reject if TS ≤ −t*. If Hₐ: μ ≠ μ₀, reject if |TS| ≥ t*. Find t* in the t-table, row df=n-1 (next smaller if not listed), column α (one-sided) or α/2 (two-sided). Decide and conclude as for a proportion: “There is / is not sufficient evidence at the α level that the mean of [variable] is [greater than / less than / different from] μ₀.”

Worked example

Is there enough oxygen for the fish? \alpha=0.05

Fish are dying in a river. Fish need a mean oxygen level of at least 5.0 ppm. A biologist sampled 8 randomly chosen days: \bar x=4.8133, s=0.1648 ppm, and the plots show no extreme skew or outliers.

  1. Hypotheses: H_0:\mu=5, H_a:\mu<5.
  2. Assumptions: no extreme skew or outliers; random days.
  3. Test statistic: t=\dfrac{4.8133-5}{0.1648/\sqrt 8}=-3.204 with df=8-1=7.
  4. Critical value: lower-tailed, α = 0.05, row df = 7, column 0.05, so t^*=1.895. Reject H₀ if TS ≤ −1.895.
  5. Decision: TS = −3.204 ≤ −1.895 is in the rejection region, so we reject H₀.
  6. Or with the p-value: P(T\le -3.204) with df = 7. In row 7, 3.204 is between 2.998 (column 0.01) and 3.499 (column 0.005), so 0.005 < p-value < 0.01; exactly, p-value = 0.0075. Either way it is below α = 0.05: reject H₀. Same decision.
  7. Conclusion: there is sufficient evidence at the 0.05 level that the mean oxygen content is less than 5.0 ppm.
TI-84 checkSTAT → TESTS → 2:T-Test → Stats: μ₀ = 5, x̄ = 4.8133, Sx = .1648, n = 8, μ < μ₀. It reports t = −3.204. invT(.05,7) = −1.895.
Excel check=(4.8133-5)/(0.1648/SQRT(8)) gives −3.204; =T.INV(0.05,7) gives −1.895.
Worked example

Nurses’ calories from fat \alpha=0.01

An SRS of 165 female nurses gave \bar x=35.294\% of calories from fat with s=6.981, and no extreme skew or outliers. Is the mean percentage different from 30?

  1. Hypotheses: H_0:\mu=30, H_a:\mu\ne 30.
  2. Test statistic: t=\dfrac{35.294-30}{6.981/\sqrt{165}}=9.741 with df=164.
  3. Critical value: two-sided, α = 0.01, column α/2 = 0.005. df = 164 is not in the table, so use the next smaller row, df = 100: t^*=2.626. Reject H₀ if |TS| ≥ 2.626.
  4. Decision: TS = 9.741 ≥ 2.626 is in the rejection region, so we reject H₀.
  5. Or with the p-value: in row df = 100, 9.741 is beyond the last column (0.0005), so the two-sided p-value is less than 2(0.0005) = 0.001, below α = 0.01: reject H₀. Same decision.
  6. Conclusion: there is sufficient evidence at the 0.01 level that the average percentage of calories from fat is different from 30.
Worked example

Is entry-level pay at least $500 a week? \alpha=0.05

Management claims the mean weekly income of entry-level assembly-line workers is at least $500. A random sample of 9 workers gives \bar x=\$441.11 and s=\$12.69, with no extreme skew or outliers.

  1. Hypotheses: H_0:\mu=500, H_a:\mu<500.
  2. Test statistic: t=\dfrac{441.11-500}{12.69/\sqrt 9}=-13.922 with df=8.
  3. Critical value: lower-tailed, α = 0.05, row df = 8, column 0.05, so t^*=1.860. Reject H₀ if TS ≤ −1.860.
  4. Decision: TS = −13.922 ≤ −1.860 is in the rejection region, so we reject H₀.
  5. Or with the p-value: in row df = 8, 13.922 is beyond the last column, so the p-value is less than 0.0005, below α = 0.05: reject H₀. Same decision.
  6. Conclusion: there is sufficient evidence at the 0.05 level that the true mean income of entry-level assembly-line workers is less than $500 per week, contradicting management’s claim.
Summary

Every test follows the same steps

  1. Hypotheses, stated before gathering data; the new theory or claim goes in Hₐ.
  2. Assumptions: normality and independence.
  3. Test statistic: (estimate − hypothesized value) / standard error.
  4. Decide, either way. Critical value: from the t-table (z* row for a proportion, row df = n − 1 for a mean; column α one-sided or α/2 two-sided); reject H₀ if the TS falls in the rejection region. p-value: the area beyond the TS in the direction of Hₐ (doubled for ≠); reject H₀ if p-value ≤ α.
  5. Decision: reject or fail to reject H₀. Both methods give the same decision.
  6. Conclusion in context, in terms of whether there was enough evidence for Hₐ.
Common mistakes. Using df = n instead of n − 1. Rounding df up to the next listed row (always use the next smaller df). Comparing a negative TS with +t* in a lower-tailed test. Using z* for a mean when σ is unknown. Forgetting to double a two-sided p-value.

6. Errors, α, β, and power

DecisionH₀ is trueH₀ is false
Reject H₀Type I errorCorrect decision
Fail to reject H₀Correct decisionType II error
  • Type I error: rejecting H₀ when H₀ is true. \alpha=P(\text{Type I error}), the significance level. It is usually considered more serious, so we set α before running the test.
  • Type II error: failing to reject H₀ when H₀ is false. \beta=P(\text{Type II error}).
  • α and β are inversely related: lowering one raises the other. Increase n to lower both at once.
  • Setting α = 0.05 means that when H₀ is true there is a 5% chance of drawing a sample whose TS lands in the rejection region.
Worked example

Which error could we have made?

  1. We test H_0:\mu=30 vs H_a:\mu\ne 30 and the TS falls in the rejection region, so we reject H₀. If we later learn that μ = 30, H₀ was true: that decision was a Type I error.
  2. Therapeutic touch: we failed to reject H₀ at α = 0.01. The possible error is Type II (failing to reject a false H₀). Consequence: people would not seek care that could actually help.
  3. Nurses’ fat intake: we rejected H₀ at α = 0.01. The possible error is Type I (rejecting a true H₀). Consequence: we would wrongly conclude the nurses’ average fat intake differs from 30% when it does not.

Planning a study: power

When choosing a sample size, consider the significance level (how much protection against a Type I error), the effect size (how large a difference matters in practice: |p_a-p_0| for a proportion, |\mu_a-\mu_0| for a mean), and the power (how sure we want to be of detecting that effect).

\text{Power}=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ is false})=1-\beta

The power against a specific alternative is the probability the test rejects H₀ at the chosen α when that alternative is true. It is the probability of a correct decision, so higher is better. If a test has power 0.98 and H₀ is false, 98% of all samples lead us to reject H₀. Software calculates power, so we focus on interpreting it.

Worked example

Does diet cola lose sweetness?

Tasters score a cola’s sweetness from 1 to 10, then again after four months of storage. The differences (before − after) show whether sweetness is lost.

  1. Hypotheses: H_0:\mu=0 vs H_a:\mu>0, where μ is the mean loss in sweetness score.
  2. Significance level: the manufacturer accepts a Type I error up to 5% of the time (α = 0.05).
  3. Effect size: a mean loss of 0.8 points will be noticed by consumers.
  4. Power: they want to be 90% sure the test detects a mean loss of 0.8, so probability at least 0.9 that a test at α = 0.05 rejects H₀ when \mu_a=0.8. Software then gives the number of tasters needed.
  5. \beta=1-\text{Power}=1-0.9=0.1, because when H₀ is false the only two outcomes are rejecting (power) and failing to reject (β).
Worked example

Caffeine

You plan to test whether college students consume more than 25 mg of caffeine per week on average, with an SRS of 64 students. If the true mean is 30 mg, the test has power 0.85.

  1. Hypotheses: H_0:\mu=25 vs H_a:\mu>25.
  2. Significance level α = 0.05 (the default); effect size |\mu_a-\mu_0|=30-25=5; power = P(reject a false H₀) = 0.85.
  3. Interpretation: if the true mean is 30 mg, 85% of all possible samples of size 64 would lead us to reject H₀.
  4. \beta=1-0.85=0.15.

Ways to increase power: increase α, decrease β, increase the sample size n (what we typically do), or increase the effect size. The picture: the H₀ distribution of the statistic is split at the critical value into “correct, 1 − α” and “Type I error, α”; the Hₐ distribution is split at the same line into “Type II error, β” and “correct, power = 1 − β”. The lab below lets you watch this happen.

7. Significance, usefulness, and CIs

A test has statistical significance when it leads us to reject H₀ in favor of Hₐ, that is, when the test statistic falls in the rejection region. Every decision to reject is statistically significant.

A rejection has practical significance only if the difference between the statistic and the hypothesized value, |\hat p-p_0| or |\bar x-\mu_0|, is large enough to matter: large enough to influence real decisions and meaningful to the people affected. Not every statistically significant result is practically significant.

Worked example

Is the faster headache pill worth it?

Brand A costs $0.10 per pill and Brand B $1.50. A randomized experiment with 2,000 volunteers found mean time to relief 10.06 minutes for A and 9.95 minutes for B, and the test statistic fell in the rejection region at α = 0.05.

  1. Hypotheses: H_0:\mu_A=\mu_B vs H_a:\mu_A\ne\mu_B (μ = mean time to relief for each brand).
  2. Statistically significant? Yes: the TS fell in the rejection region at α = 0.05.
  3. Practically significant? Would you pay $1.40 more per pill for relief 0.11 minutes (6.6 seconds) sooner? No.
  4. Why was it significant? The sample was very large. Extremely large samples almost always give extreme test statistics and a decision to reject H₀.

Confidence intervals and two-sided tests

Both rely on the sampling distribution of x̄ or p̂. A two-sided test at level α can be carried out with a confidence interval at level C=1-\alpha:

  • If the interval contains the hypothesized value, it is plausible: fail to reject H₀.
  • If the interval does not contain it, it is not plausible: reject H₀.
Worked example

Same decision, two ways

  1. Nurses: testing H_0:\mu=30 vs H_a:\mu\ne 30 at α = 0.01 gave TS = 9.741 with df = 164. Reject, because |TS| = 9.741 ≥ t* = 2.626. The 99% confidence interval for μ is (33.877, 36.710). It does not contain 30, so 30 is not plausible and we reject H₀: the same decision.
  2. Testing H_0:p=0.4 vs H_a:p\ne 0.4, a 90% CI for p is (0.28, 0.42). At α = 0.10 the interval contains 0.4, so 0.4 is plausible and we fail to reject H₀. The test statistic will therefore not fall in the rejection region: |TS| < z* = 1.645.
Summary

Uses and abuses

  • Not every rejection is practically significant. Statistical significance: the TS falls in the rejection region. Practical significance: a difference large enough to matter.
  • \alpha=P(\text{Type I error})=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ true}); \beta=P(\text{Type II error})=P(\text{FTR }H_0\mid H_0\text{ false}). They are inversely related; increase n to lower both.
  • \text{Power}=1-\beta=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ false}). To increase power: increase α, n or the effect size; decrease β.
  • A confidence interval is equivalent to a two-sided test: hypothesized value inside the interval, fail to reject; outside, reject.
Read the strength of the claim. Failing to reject does not show H₀ is true; the test may simply lack power. Do not choose hypotheses or α after seeing the data, keep testing until something lands in the rejection region, or report only favorable results.
