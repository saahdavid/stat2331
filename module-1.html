Data Distributions

Describe what you see. Learn to measure center, spread, shape, and unusual values.

1. From data to inference

Statistics is the art and science of learning from data. It includes designing studies and analyzing data objectively, as well as calculating numbers. Four phases connect a research question to a defensible conclusion: research question → design → description → inference.

  • Identify subjects, the target population, the sample frame, and the sample.
  • Distinguish categorical and quantitative variables, parameters and statistics.
  • Describe a distribution’s shape, center, spread, and unusual observations.
  • Calculate proportions, means, medians, sample SD, quartiles, and outlier fences.

Subjects are the units we observe. The target population is everyone we want to understand. The sample frame is the list from which we select. The sample is the subset for which we obtain data. A parameter summarizes the population; a statistic summarizes the sample. Probability uses a known population to reason about samples; statistical inference uses a sample to learn about an unknown population.

Worked example

A new diet plan

A doctor samples 30 dieting obese women following a plan and 30 not following it from her clinic list. Mean losses after six weeks are 18 and 12 pounds.

  1. The subjects are the women. The target population is all dieting obese women.
  2. The sample frame is the clinic list; the sample is the 60 selected women.
  3. The two sample means, 18 and 12, are statistics. Population means are unknown parameters.
  4. The clinic list may not represent the entire target population. A large sample cannot correct an unrepresentative frame.

2. Variables and displays

A variable is a characteristic recorded for each subject. Categorical values identify groups: nominal categories have no natural order (cookie brand); ordinal categories do (low, medium, high). Quantitative values have meaningful numerical operations: discrete values are countable; continuous measurements can take values throughout an interval. Whole-year recorded age is discrete at that recording precision; underlying elapsed age is continuous.

For categorical data, begin with a frequency table, then a bar chart (separate bars) or pie chart. For quantitative data, use a histogram (adjacent numerical bins), stem-and-leaf plot, or boxplot. Describe the number of modes, symmetry or skewness, center, spread, and outliers. A right-skewed distribution has a longer right tail; a left-skewed one has a longer left tail. “Skew” names the tail, not where most observations lie.

\hat p=\frac{X}{n},\qquad \text{percentage}=100\hat p\%
Worked example

Shark attacks

The four state counts are California 187, Florida 828, Hawaii 230, Texas 58; total 1303.

  1. Choose the category of interest: Florida, with X = 828.
  2. Divide by the total: p̂ = 828/1303 = 0.6355, about 63.5%.
  3. Distinguish the categorical variable “state of an attack” from the quantitative variable “number of attacks in a state.” The way the data are recorded determines the variable.
TI-84828 ÷ 1303 ENTER
Excel=828/1303. Format as Percentage if desired.
Watch out. Numbers are not always quantitative. A zip code or a jersey number is a label, so it is categorical. And a variable like "age group: 18–24, 25–34" is ordinal, even though it is built from numbers.

3. Center: mean and median

The mean uses every observation; the median is the midpoint of the ordered data; the mode is the most frequent value. For odd n, take the middle ordered value for the median. For even n, average the two middle values. The population mean is μ; the sample mean is x̄.

\bar x=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i}{n}
Worked example

One outlier changes the mean

  1. For 1, 2, 3, 4, 5: x̄ = 15/5 = 3 and median = 3.
  2. Replace 5 with 100: the sum is 110, so x̄ = 110/5 = 22.
  3. The ordered middle observation remains 3. The median is resistant; the mean is pulled toward the outlier.
TI-84STAT → EDIT → enter values in L1. STAT → CALC → 1-Var Stats L1. Read x̄ and Med.
Excel=AVERAGE(1,2,3,4,100) gives 22; =MEDIAN(1,2,3,4,100) gives 3.

For a roughly symmetric, unimodal distribution, mean and median are close. In right-skewed data the mean is usually larger; in left-skewed data it is usually smaller. This is a useful guide, not a universal theorem for every possible data set.

Common mistakes. Forgetting to sort before finding the median. With an even n, picking one of the two middle values instead of averaging them.

4. Spread: work out s by hand

The range is maximum minus minimum. Sample variance measures squared deviations from the sample mean; sample SD is its square root, in the original units. All values equal gives s = 0. Greater spread usually produces larger s.

s^2=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1},\qquad s=\sqrt{s^2}
Worked example

SD for 1, 2, 3, 4, 5

  1. Mean: x̄ = (1+2+3+4+5)/5 = 3.
  2. Deviations: −2, −1, 0, 1, 2.
  3. Square and add: 4+1+0+1+4 = 10.
  4. Divide by n−1 = 4: s² = 10/4 = 2.5.
  5. Take the square root: s = √2.5 = 1.5811.
TI-841-Var Stats L1: Sx = 1.5811. σx uses n and is a population SD.
Excel=STDEV.S(A1:A5); variance: =VAR.S(A1:A5).
Red-pencil reminder. Divide by n−1 for a sample. Do not report variance when asked for SD. Neither the mean nor SD is resistant to outliers.
Watch out. The TI shows both Sx and σx. In this course you want Sx, which divides by n-1.

5. Quartiles, IQR, and outliers

Order the observations. Find the overall median. Divide the data into a lower half and an upper half, excluding the overall median from both halves when n is odd. Q1 is the median of the lower half; Q3 is the median of the upper half. This is our TI-84 convention.

IQR=Q_3-Q_1,\quad \text{fences}=Q_1-1.5IQR,\;Q_3+1.5IQR
Worked example

The five-number summary

1, 2, 7, 11, 12, 18, 23, 25, 98, 197

  1. Median = (12+18)/2 = 15.
  2. Lower half: 1, 2, 7, 11, 12, giving Q1 = 7. Upper half: 18, 23, 25, 98, 197, giving Q3 = 25.
  3. Five-number summary: (1, 7, 15, 25, 197). IQR = 25−7 = 18.
  4. Mild fences: 7−1.5(18) = −20 and 25+1.5(18) = 52.
  5. Extreme upper fence: 25+3(18) = 79. Both 98 and 197 are extreme outliers.
  6. Boxplot whiskers reach the most extreme non-outliers: 1 and 25. Plot the outliers separately.
TI-841-Var Stats → minX, Q1, Med, Q3, maxX. 2nd Y= → modified boxplot.
ExcelSort A1:A10. =MEDIAN(A1:A5) and =MEDIAN(A6:A10). Excel QUARTILE.INC/EXC may use a different convention.
About outliers. Never delete an outlier just because it is extreme. Fix it if it is a typo; otherwise keep it in every calculation. If you report results without it, say so and say why.

6. Put the description together

Describe shape, center, spread, and outliers in context, including units. For skewed data use median and IQR; for reasonably symmetric data mean and SD are useful. Investigate an outlier before changing or removing it. Correct a recording mistake if verified; never remove a legitimate extreme value simply because it is inconvenient.
