1. A claim, then evidence

A hypothesis concerns a population parameter. The null H₀ states no effect or a reference value; the alternative Hₐ states the research direction. Choose hypotheses and significance α before examining the data. A test weighs evidence; it does not prove a hypothesis.

  • Write hypotheses and check assumptions for a single proportion or mean.
  • Calculate a test statistic and obtain an appropriate one- or two-sided p-value.
  • Make a decision, then state a contextual conclusion in terms of Hₐ.
  • Explain Type I/II errors, power, practical significance, and CI connections.
H_0:p=p_0\quad\text{or}\quad H_0:\mu=\mu_0;\qquad H_a:\; <,\; >,\;\ne

H₀ always uses equality in our calculations. “At least 5” opposed by “less than 5” uses H₀: μ=5 and Hₐ: μ<5. Use parameters p and μ, not statistics p̂ and x̄.

TS=\frac{\text{estimate}-\text{hypothesized value}}{\text{null standard error}}

A p-value is the probability, assuming H₀ is true, of a test statistic at least as extreme as observed in the direction specified by Hₐ. It is not P(H₀ is true) and is not simply the probability of the exact observed sample.

Claims with "at least" or "at most". Management claims mean income is at least $500. That is the status quo, so it goes in H_0: \mu = 500, and the suspicion goes in H_a: \mu < 500. In this course H_0 is always written with "=".

2. Test a single proportion

z=\frac{\hat p-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}

Check random, approximately independent observations and expected counts np₀≥10 and n(1−p₀)≥10. Both the normality check and the test SE use p₀ because the calculation assumes H₀ is true.

Worked example

52 correct identifications out of 100

  1. Define p as the true probability of a correct identification. H₀: p=.5; Hₐ: p>.5; α=.01.
  2. Under H₀, expected successes and failures are both 50. The lecture model treats randomized trials as independent; repeated responses within practitioners would require care in a real analysis.
  3. p̂=52/100=.52; null SE=√[.5(.5)/100]=.05.
  4. z=(.52−.5)/.05=.40.
  5. Right-tail p-value=1−Φ(.40)=1−.6554=.3446.
  6. .3446>.01: fail to reject H₀.
  7. There is insufficient evidence at the .01 level that correct identification occurs more than half the time.
TI-84STAT → TESTS → 1-PropZTest. p₀=.5, x=52, n=100; select >. Or normalcdf(.4,1E99,0,1).
Excel=1-NORM.S.DIST((52/100-0.5)/SQRT(0.5*0.5/100),TRUE).
Use p₀ in the test. A CI uses p̂ in its SE. A test assumes H₀, so its SE uses p₀. Failing to reject does not mean “accept H₀.”
Worked example

Is "7" chosen 10% of the time? \alpha = 0.05

69 of 189 people asked to pick a number from 1 to 10 chose 7. H_0: p = 0.1 vs H_a: p \ne 0.1. Conditions: 18.9 and 170.1, both at least 10.

z = (0.3651 - 0.1)/\sqrt{0.1(0.9)/189} = 12.15. The p-value 2P(Z \ge 12.15) is essentially 0, so reject H_0: the chance of choosing 7 is not 10%.

3. Tail, decision, conclusion

p_{>}=1-\Phi(TS),\quad p_{<}=\Phi(TS),\quad p_{\ne}=2[1-\Phi(|TS|)]

For a right-sided alternative use the right tail even if TS is negative; it then gives a large p-value. For a left-sided alternative use the left tail. For a two-sided alternative, double the tail beyond |TS|. Sketch the picture before consulting the table.

p\text{-value}\le\alpha\Rightarrow\text{reject }H_0;\qquad p\text{-value}>\alpha\Rightarrow\text{fail to reject }H_0

Use: “There is/is not sufficient evidence at the α level that [population parameter in context] is [direction from Hₐ] [null value].” The conclusion concerns the population, not whether the observed statistic is greater or smaller.

Worked example

Is a proportion below one half?

  1. n=1014, p̂=.48. H₀: p=.50; Hₐ: p<.50.
  2. Null SE=√[.5(.5)/1014]; z≈−1.2737.
  3. Table z=−1.27 gives .1020; software gives .1014.
  4. Both exceed α=.05. Fail to reject. The sample proportion below .5 alone is not sufficient evidence of a population minority.
TI-84normalcdf(−1E99,−1.2737,0,1).
Excel=NORM.S.DIST(-1.2737,TRUE).
Common mistakes. Using \hat p in the SE. Forgetting to double the tail for a two-sided test. Reporting the table value (left area) when H_a is ">".

4. Test a single mean with t

t=\frac{\bar x-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n},\qquad df=n-1

Check random data, approximate independence, and no extreme skew or extreme outliers. Use the t table to bracket the p-value. Pick the next smaller listed df, locate |t| between critical values, and read their upper-tail probabilities. Double both bounds for a two-sided test. If the statistic lies in the opposite direction from a one-sided alternative, complement the small-tail bracket instead.

Worked example

Is mean oxygen below 5 ppm?

  1. H₀: μ=5; Hₐ: μ<5; α=.05. n=8, x̄=4.8133, s=.1648. The sample has no extreme skew or outliers.
  2. SE=.1648/√8=.05827…
  3. t=(4.8133−5)/.05827…≈−3.204; df=7.
  4. At df=7, upper-tail .01 has critical value 2.998 and .005 has 3.499.
  5. By symmetry, .005<p<.01 for the left tail. Exact software gives about .0075.
  6. The entire bracket is below .05: reject H₀. There is sufficient evidence that mean oxygen is below 5 ppm.
TI-84STAT → TESTS → T-Test → Stats; μ₀=5, x̄=4.8133, Sx=.1648, n=8, alternative <. Or tcdf(−1E99,−3.204,7).
Excel=T.DIST((4.8133-5)/(0.1648/SQRT(8)),7,TRUE).

For the nurses’ two-sided test, x̄=35.294, s=6.981, n=165, μ₀=30 gives t≈9.741. The hand table uses df=100 and puts the two-sided p below .001; exact software is much smaller. If a table bracket straddles α, the table alone does not settle the decision: obtain greater precision.

5. Errors, α, β, and power

DecisionH₀ trueH₀ false
Reject H₀Type I errorCorrect: detection
Fail to reject H₀CorrectType II error
\alpha=P(\text{reject}\mid H_0\text{ true}),\quad\beta=P(\text{fail to reject}\mid H_a\text{ true}),\quad\text{power}=1-\beta

Power is defined at a specified alternative value. A test with power .90 at a given effect size detects that effect in about 90% of repeated samples; β=.10. Power rises with larger sample size, larger effect, or larger α, all else fixed. Increasing α increases the Type I error risk. Increasing n can improve detection while holding α fixed.

Worked example

Detecting loss of sweetness

  1. Define the difference as before minus after. H₀: μ=0; Hₐ: μ>0.
  2. A meaningful loss is .8 points. Plan for α=.05 and power .90 at μ=.8.
  3. Then β=1−.90=.10 at that alternative. It is not the probability H₀ is false.
TI-841−.9 gives β=.1. Power planning also needs an estimate of variability.
Excel=1-0.9. Always state the effect size associated with the power.
More worked examples
  • We failed to reject H_0 in the therapeutic-touch study. Possible error: Type II. Consequence: people might skip a treatment that actually helps.
  • We rejected H_0 in the fat-intake study. Possible error: Type I. Consequence: concluding nurses' mean fat intake differs from 30% when it doesn't.
Worked example

Caffeine

Testing H_0: \mu = 25 vs H_a: \mu > 25 mg with n = 64 at \alpha = 0.05, the power when \mu = 30 is 0.85. Interpretation: if the true mean is 30 mg, 85% of all samples of size 64 would lead us to reject H_0. Effect size: 30 - 25 = 5. \beta = 1 - 0.85 = 0.15.

6. Significance, usefulness, and CIs

Statistical significance means the test rejects at the stated α. Practical significance asks whether the effect is large enough to matter. A large sample can make a small effect statistically detectable; a significant result is not automatically useful, causal, or well measured.

In the headache-pill example, Brand B costs $1.50 versus $.10 and relieves pain in 9.95 versus 10.06 minutes. The .11-minute gain is 6.6 seconds. A p-value of .045 is statistically significant at .05; deciding whether that gain is worth $1.40 more requires a practical judgment.

For a matching two-sided mean t test, use the (1−α) CI: if μ₀ is inside, fail to reject; if outside, reject. The nurses’ 99% exact interval (33.877,36.710) excludes 30, matching rejection at .01. This equivalence requires matching procedures. A Wald proportion CI using p̂ and a score z test using p₀ can differ near the boundary; do not treat their numerical equivalence as exact.

Read the strength of the claim. A p-value is conditional on H₀, not the probability H₀ is true. A failure to reject may reflect limited power. Do not choose hypotheses or α after seeing the data, repeatedly test until p becomes small, or report only favorable outcomes.
