Normal & Binomial Distributions

Turn a bell curve or a count of successes into a probability, one step at a time.

Practice this module →

1. Recognize a normal model

A normal distribution is symmetric, unimodal, and bell-shaped. We write X\sim N(\mu,\sigma), where the second parameter is the standard deviation, not the variance. The curve has total area one. Use a histogram, boxplot, and normal Q-Q plot to assess whether a normal model is reasonable. Q-Q points should roughly follow a straight line; strong curves, gaps, or outliers raise concerns.

  • Apply the Empirical Rule and standardize values.
  • Use a left-area table for normal probabilities and percentiles.
  • Check BINS, calculate binomial probabilities, and use a normal approximation when appropriate.
68\%:\mu\pm\sigma,\qquad95\%:\mu\pm2\sigma,\qquad99.7\%:\mu\pm3\sigma
From lecture · Example 1, p. 7

Exam scores: μ=80, σ=5

  1. Within one SD: (75,85), about 68%.
  2. Within two SDs: (70,90), about 95%.
  3. Within three SDs: (65,95), about 99.7%.
  4. P(X>75)=.5+.68/2=.84 using the Empirical Rule.
  5. P(70<X<85)=.95/2+.68/2=.815.
TI-84The Empirical Rule is approximate. 2nd VARS → normalcdf(75,1E99,80,5) gives the exact model area, about .8413.
Excel=1-NORM.DIST(75,80,5,TRUE). A small difference from .84 is expected.

2. Standardize before the table

z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma},\qquad Z\sim N(0,1)

A z-score tells how many SDs an observation lies above (positive) or below (negative) its mean. Standardizing preserves the corresponding area. Draw the curve, mark the original cutoff, calculate z, then place that z under the cutoff.

From lecture · Examples 2–3, pp. 8–12

Adult female heights

Heights are approximately N(65,3.5). Find P(X<68).

  1. z=(68−65)/3.5=.857142…
  2. Round z to .86 for the table.
  3. Find row .8, column .06. The left area is .8051.
  4. Software uses unrounded z and gives .8043. Both answers represent the same method at different precision.
TI-842nd VARS → normalcdf(−1E99,68,65,3.5).
Excel=NORM.DIST(68,65,3.5,TRUE). TRUE requests cumulative left area.
Worked example

Heights

Women's heights are N(65, 3.5) and men's are N(70, 4) in inches.

  • A 72-inch woman: z = (72-65)/3.5 = 2, two standard deviations above average.
  • A 62-inch man: z = (62-70)/4 = -2, two standard deviations below average.
  • A 67-inch man: z = (67-70)/4 = -0.75. The farther z is from 0, the more unusual the value.

3. Four normal-area patterns

Let Φ(z) denote the left area from the normal table. Round z to two decimal places and use four-decimal entries. Beyond ±3.49, the table area is essentially zero or one.

P(Z>a)=1-\Phi(a),\quad P(a<Z<b)=\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)
P(Z<a\text{ or }Z>b)=\Phi(a)+1-\Phi(b)
From lecture · Example 4, pp. 14–15

Dragonfly wingspans: N(4,.25)

  1. Less than 4.3: z=(4.3−4)/.25=1.20; area=.8849.
  2. More than 4.3: 1−.8849=.1151.
  3. Between 3.6 and 4.7: z₁=−1.60, z₂=2.80; .9974−.0548=.9426.
  4. Outside 3.8 and 4.2: z=±.80; .2119+(1−.7881)=.4238. Software gives .4237.
TI-84normalcdf(lower,upper,4,.25). For outside, use 1−normalcdf(3.8,4.2,4,.25).
ExcelBetween: =NORM.DIST(4.7,4,0.25,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(3.6,4,0.25,TRUE). Outside: 1 minus the between area.
Draw first. A “greater than” question usually needs a complement. A “between” question needs subtraction of two left areas. Do not read one table entry and stop.
Worked example

Women shorter than 68 inches

  1. P(X < 68) with X \sim N(65, 3.5).
  2. z = (68-65)/3.5 = 0.857\ldots \approx 0.86.
  3. Row 0.8, column .06: P(Z<0.86) = 0.8051.

Software gives 0.8043. The small difference comes from rounding z; either is accepted in practice here.

Common mistakes. Reporting the table value when the question asks for "greater than." Subtracting in the wrong order for "between" (the answer can't be negative). Forgetting to standardize and looking up x itself in the table.

4. Work backward: inverse normal

When an area is given, first express it as an area to the left. Search the inside of the table, recover z from its row and column, then return to the original units. If an area falls exactly halfway between two entries, use the midpoint of the corresponding z values.

x=\mu+z\sigma
From lecture · pp. 16–17

A 95th percentile, and the middle 90%

  1. For dragonfly N(4,.25), the 95th percentile has left area .95.
  2. .9500 is halfway between .9495 at z=1.64 and .9505 at z=1.65. Use z=1.645.
  3. x=4+1.645(.25)=4.41125, about 4.411 inches.
  4. The middle 90% leaves 5% in each tail. Use ±1.645, giving (3.589,4.411).
TI-84invNorm(.95,4,.25); lower endpoint invNorm(.05,4,.25).
Excel=NORM.INV(0.95,4,0.25) and =NORM.INV(0.05,4,0.25).

5. Is it binomial? Check BINS

B: binary outcome on each trial. I: independent trials. N: a fixed number n of trials. S: the same success probability p on every trial. “Success” simply names the category being counted. Write X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p).

Sampling without replacement from a small population violates independence and constant p. Our course rule of thumb is population size at least 20 times sample size. Do not confuse population N with sample n.

P(X=k)=\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
From lecture · Example 6, p. 27

A weighted coin

A coin has P(heads)=.75. Toss it independently four times.

  1. BINS holds: two outcomes, independent tosses, fixed n=4, fixed p=.75.
  2. For exactly three heads, C(4,3)=4!/(3!1!)=4.
  3. P(X=3)=4(.75)³(.25)=.421875≈.4219.
TI-842nd VARS → binompdf(4,.75,3). To calculate C(4,3), enter 4 MATH → PRB → nCr → 3.
Excel=COMBIN(4,3)*0.75^3*0.25 or =BINOM.DIST(3,4,0.75,FALSE).

Translate words carefully: “at most k” is X≤k; “fewer than k” is X≤k−1; “at least k” is 1−P(X≤k−1); “more than k” is 1−P(X≤k). Add exact-probability terms or use the complement to save work.

From lecture · Examples 7–8, pp. 28–30

At most one, and at least three

  1. For Binomial(10,.5), P(X≤1)=C(10,0)(.5)¹⁰+C(10,1)(.5)¹⁰=.0009766+.0097656=.0107.
  2. For Binomial(30,.11), P(X<3)=P(0)+P(1)+P(2)≈.3442.
  3. P(X≥3)=1−P(X≤2)≈.6558.
TI-84binomcdf(10,.5,1); 1−binomcdf(30,.11,2).
Excel=BINOM.DIST(1,10,0.5,TRUE); =1-BINOM.DIST(2,30,0.11,TRUE).
Small populations break independence. Choosing 6 of your 20 friends, 4 of them left-handed: the chance of a lefty is 4/20 on the first pick but 3/19 or 4/19 on the next. Rule of thumb: the sample should be less than 5% of the population (population at least 20 times the sample).
Worked example

Malnutrition, n = 30, p = 0.11

  1. P(X=0) = (0.89)^{30} = 0.0303.
  2. P(X=1) = 30(0.11)(0.89)^{29} = 0.1124.
  3. P(X=2) = \binom{30}{2}(0.11)^2(0.89)^{28} = 435(0.0121)(0.0383) = 0.2015.
  4. Fewer than 3: 0.0303 + 0.1124 + 0.2015 = 0.3442. At least 3: 1 - 0.3442 = 0.6558.
Keep four decimals (or more) in each term and round only at the end. Your calculator's MATH PRB 3:nCr computes \binom{n}{k}: type 30 nCr 2.

6. Binomial center, spread, approximation

\mu=np,\quad\sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)},\quad X\approx N\!\left(np,\sqrt{np(1-p)}\right)

Use the normal approximation only when np≥10 and n(1−p)≥10. Our course uses no continuity correction: standardize the stated count directly. If the conditions fail, use the exact binomial model.

From lecture · Example 9, pp. 35–36

A very unusual count

  1. For n=262 and p=.422, np=110.564 and n(1−p)=151.436, so both conditions pass.
  2. μ=110.564 and σ=√[262(.422)(.578)]=7.994…
  3. For at least 207, z=(207−110.564)/7.994…≈12.06.
  4. This is beyond the table: the right-tail area is essentially zero (less than .0002 by the table).
TI-84normalcdf(207,1E99,110.564,√(262*.422*.578)).
Excel=1-NORM.DIST(207,110.564,SQRT(262*0.422*0.578),TRUE). Extremely small tails may display as zero.
