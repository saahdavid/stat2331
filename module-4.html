Probability Basics

Make sense of chance with tables, trees, complements, and probability models.

1. A language for chance

A random phenomenon has unpredictable individual outcomes but regular long-run behavior. Its sample space S contains all possible outcomes. An event is a subset of those outcomes. For equally likely outcomes, probability is favorable outcomes divided by total outcomes; do not use this counting rule if outcomes are not equally likely.

  • Translate “and,” “or,” “not,” and “given” into event operations.
  • Use tables and trees for joint, conditional, and reversed probabilities.
  • Distinguish independence from disjointness and handle sampling without replacement.
  • Find discrete means and SDs, and continuous uniform areas.

The Law of Large Numbers says repeated relative frequencies tend toward the theoretical probability. It does not promise steady improvement at every trial or guarantee the next outcome will compensate for earlier outcomes.

2. The probability rules

A union A∪B means A or B or both. An intersection A∩B means both. The complement Aᶜ means not A. Disjoint (mutually exclusive) events cannot occur together.

0\le P(A)\le1,\quad P(S)=1,\quad P(A^c)=1-P(A)
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)

If events are disjoint, the intersection is zero. Otherwise subtract the overlap so it is counted once.

Worked example

Diabetes treatment and complications

Let A = treatment 1 and B = complications.

  1. P(A)=88/200=.44; P(B)=20/200=.10.

3. “Given” changes the denominator

Conditioning restricts the sample space to an event already known to have occurred. The order matters: P(B|A) generally differs from P(A|B).

P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)},\quad P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)
Worked example

A positive screening test

Of 5282 screened pregnancies, 48 affected and 1307 unaffected pregnancies test positive. Six affected and 3921 unaffected test negative.

  1. We are given a positive test, so the denominator is all positives: 48+1307=1355.
  2. P(affected | positive)=48/1355=.0354, about 3.54%.
  3. Reversing the condition gives P(positive | affected)=48/54=.8889. This is a different probability.
TI-8448÷1355, then compare 48÷54.
Excel=48/(48+1307); reversed: =48/(48+6).
Watch the denominator. A false positive is a positive result when the condition is absent. “Probability of a positive given no condition” differs from “probability of no condition given a positive.” Always name the condition after the vertical bar.
Common mistakes. Forgetting to subtract P(A\cap B) in the addition rule. Using the wrong denominator in a conditional probability: in P(B\mid A), the denominator is the "given" group A.

4. Independence and repeated trials

A\perp B\iff P(B\mid A)=P(B)\iff P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)

Independent events do not change one another’s probabilities. Disjoint positive-probability events are dependent: knowing one occurred rules out the other.

Worked example

Three unusual subjects

Five of 100 people are unusual. Draw three without replacement.

  1. First unusual: 5/100.
  2. Given that success, second unusual: 4/99.
  3. Given both successes, third unusual: 3/98.
  4. P(all three)=(5/100)(4/99)(3/98)=.0000618429.
TI-84(5/100)(4/99)(3/98).
Excel=(5/100)*(4/99)*(3/98).

For independent trials with success chance p, “at least one” is easiest through its complement:

P(\text{at least one success})=1-(1-p)^n
TI-84For p=.2, n=4: 1−(1−.2)^4 gives .5904.
Excel=1-(1-0.2)^4.

5. Multiply branches, then add paths

On a probability tree, probabilities leaving each node sum to 1. Multiply probabilities along a path for its joint probability. Add disjoint paths that lead to the desired outcome. To reverse a condition, divide a joint probability by the total of all paths into the new condition.

Worked example

Skin cancer by location

Location probabilities are head .15, trunk .41, limbs .44. The respective probabilities of woman given location are .56, .37, .80.

  1. P(head and woman)=.15(.56)=.084.
  2. P(woman)=.15(.56)+.41(.37)+.44(.80)=.5877.
  3. For a reversed condition, P(head | woman)=.084/.5877≈.1429.
TI-84Store the branch products, add for P(woman), then divide .084 by that sum.
Excel=0.15*0.56+0.41*0.37+0.44*0.8; reverse with =0.084/0.5877.

6. Discrete and continuous models

A discrete random variable X has countable outcomes; add their probabilities for an event. Its probability model must have nonnegative probabilities summing to one. Its mean is a weighted average, not an unweighted mean of listed outcomes.

\mu_X=\sum xp(x),\quad\sigma_X^2=\sum(x-\mu_X)^2p(x),\quad\sigma_X=\sqrt{\sigma_X^2}
Worked example

Impaired ears in Dalmatians

P(X=0)=.70, P(X=1)=.22, P(X=2)=.08.

  1. μX=0(.70)+1(.22)+2(.08)=.38 ears.
  2. σX²=(0−.38)²(.70)+(1−.38)²(.22)+(2−.38)²(.08)=.3956.
  3. σX=√.3956=.6290 ears.
TI-84Outcomes in L1, probabilities in L2 → 1-Var Stats L1,L2. Use σx, not Sx, for the probability model.
Excel=SUMPRODUCT(outcomes,probabilities). Compute weighted squared deviations and take SQRT.

A continuous random variable assigns probabilities to intervals as areas under a density curve. Total area is one. P(X=c)=0 at any single point, so endpoint inclusion does not change an interval probability. For a uniform model on [a,b], height is 1/(b−a).

P(c<X<d)=\frac{d-c}{b-a}\quad(a\le c<d\le b)
Worked example

Uniform from 0 to 5

  1. Height=1/5=.2.
  2. P(1≤X≤3)=(3−1)(.2)=.4.
  3. P(X<1 or X>3)=1−.4=.6.
TI-84(3−1)÷(5−0).
Excel=(3-1)/(5-0).

When combining independent random variables, their variances add for sums or differences. If independence fails, covariance must also be considered.

Common mistakes. Forgetting to multiply by p_i when finding the mean or variance. Reporting the variance when the standard deviation was asked. Treating X > 2 as if it included 2.
